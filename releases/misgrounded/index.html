Misgrounded, catching true facts filed under the wrong exhibit.

Does each fact in a lawyer's brief really sit in the exhibit it cites? An LLM judge, run twice, to find out.

github.com/hajar6883/misgrounded

Premise

A legal brief backs every fact with an exhibit number. LLMs now draft these briefs, and the tools around them already check that the cited case law exists. The facts get less attention.

The dangerous error isn't the invented fact. It's the true fact under the wrong exhibit. It reads perfectly, and it only surfaces when the judge, or the other side, opens the exhibit.

The standard LLM check, faithfulness, can't see it: it asks whether a claim is supported by the sources as a whole. True somewhere counts as true. I call these claims misgrounded.

What it does

01  extract   split the brief into claims, one per cited value
02  rules     is this exact amount, date or % in the cited exhibit?
03  pass A    LLM judge, all exhibits: is the claim true anywhere?
04  pass B    same judge, same prompt, cited exhibit only: is it true there?

Passes A, fails B: misgrounded. The rules are cheap and never hallucinate, so they go first; the judge only sees what they can't settle, and must quote its evidence word for word.

In practice

Brief

"…invoiced at €44,400 excl. VAT, paid in full (exhibit 2)."

Pass A · all exhibits · supported

€44,400 was invoiced for phase 1 and paid in full.

Pass B · exhibit 2 only · not found

The exhibit shows a total of €62,400 and says nothing about payment.

Judge explanations, translated from French. The amount lives in exhibit 4, the invoice. Exhibit 2 is the purchase order.

Benchmark

You can't measure what a checker catches without knowing where the errors are, so the test case is fictional on purpose: a failed ERP rollout written as a French commercial dispute, 10 exhibits, 55 ground-truth facts. An LLM drafted the brief; a copy got 12 planted errors.

Result
Planted errors caught (rules alone)7 / 12
Planted errors caught (rules + judge)10 / 12
False alerts on the clean brief, 32 claims1
Citations broken by inserting one exhibit14 / 25
… with stable document IDs0 / 25

The two misses are instructive: a letter that paraphrases a contract clause, accepted as proof of it, and a right date for the wrong event, which the rules confirm before the judge can object. The one false alert is an argument, not a fact.

The last two rows are a different failure: exhibit numbers are positions, not identities. Insert one document and more than half the citations silently point elsewhere.

One fictional case, one model family, 66 claims. Planted errors aren't natural ones, so recall and false alerts are measured on separate briefs and never averaged.
